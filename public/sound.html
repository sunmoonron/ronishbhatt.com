Earthbell

Somewhere on Earth, lightning strikes about 45 times a second. Every strike rings the hollow between the ground and the ionosphere like a bell, at 7.83, 14.3, 20.8, 27.3, 33.8 and 39 Hz. It has not stopped ringing in billions of years. This page lifts it five octaves into hearing and voices it from where you stand.

It is not a generator pretending to be endless. It is a microphone on something that already is.

You are at the center. Distance from the center is true distance, and the outer edge is the far side of the planet. Left and right on the map are left and right in your ears. Dashed rings are the nodes of the selected overtone: a storm sitting on one cannot ring that note for you. Click anywhere to listen from there.

How it works

Why this gets close to infinite

  • It is overheard, not generated. The notes are the planet's own resonances, and lightning has kept them sounding for billions of years. You join a piece that started before you, and it does not end when you close the tab.
  • The score is a function of time and place. Strike times come from the world clock, so everyone with this page open hears the same strikes at the same moment, each voiced from where they stand. Pick another place, or click the map, and the same storm sounds different.
  • Every timescale has its own clock, and none of them loop. Seconds: strikes. Minutes: whistles, pings and chirps. Hours: storms follow the afternoon around the planet, so the voicing turns with the Earth. A day: daylight decides which sounds can exist at all. A year: the storm belt follows the sun north and south. The Sun itself: flares raise the bell's pitch and magnetic storms wake the chorus, live.

What you hear

  • The hum is the Schumann resonance background: six partials, each weighted by Pn(cos θ), the shape of the n-th standing wave on a sphere, summed over every storm at its distance θ from you.
  • Strikes (Q-bursts) are the rare huge flashes that make the whole cavity ring at once. Which overtones reach you depends on how far away the strike was. Its direction places it left or right.
  • Whistlers happen at night. Some lightning energy escapes along a magnetic field line, loops thousands of kilometres into space and comes down in the other hemisphere. The plasma up there delays low frequencies more than high ones (arrival time grows as one over the square root of frequency), so the sound falls, high notes first. Your own field line sets how far it travels and how slowly it falls.
  • Tweeks are also night sounds: distant lightning bouncing between the ground and the ionosphere rings at the cutoff frequency c / 2h, where h is the height of the ionosphere. The pitch is the height of the night sky. At 86.7 km, two octaves down, it is exactly A=432.
  • Dawn chorus comes in the morning: electrons trapped in the radiation belts sing rising chirps, pitched by the strength of the magnetic field where they spiral. There is more of it when the magnetosphere is stirred up (high Kp).
  • Crackle and hiss are the radio clicks (sferics) of storms near you and the hiss of the plasmasphere.
  • Overhead is the one sound here that is not the Earth's electricity: the aircraft within 55 km of downtown Toronto, from public ADS-B transponder data relayed by this site. Each is played as it would sound where you listen: the blade-pass tone of its propeller or rotor, or the rumble of its jets, pitched by the Doppler shift of its velocity toward or away from you at the moment the sound left it, softened by distance and by the air, and delayed by the travel time, so a plane 10 km away is heard where it was half a minute ago. It only knows Toronto's sky. Anywhere else, it listens from downtown.

What is real and what is stretched

Real: the resonance frequencies; the Legendre weighting by distance; the sun's position, and day and night everywhere; your magnetic latitude, L-shell and the point where your field line comes back down (centred dipole); whistler dispersion; the tweek cutoff; the chorus band from the electron gyrofrequency; Kp and the solar X-ray class (NOAA SWPC, live); convective weather (Open-Meteo, live); the aircraft over Toronto, their tracks and their Doppler shift (ADS-B, live).

Stretched: everything is shifted up by whole octaves (32 times by default) so you can hear it. The real cavity rings for about a quarter of a second, here for several. Whistlers, tweeks and chorus are slowed down and lowered two octaves, like tape at quarter speed. Event rates are thinned for listening. Storms come from a lightning climatology (where it is afternoon over land, by season) blended with live convective weather, not from detected individual strikes. The aircraft engines are synthesized, and the planes stay audible farther away than they really would.

Anchored tuning moves every bell note by the same small ratio so the lowest lands on C at A=432 (259.2 Hz). The planet's chord is close to harmonics 6, 11, 16, 21 and 26 of 1.3 Hz, so anchored it becomes harmonics of 43.2 Hz.
