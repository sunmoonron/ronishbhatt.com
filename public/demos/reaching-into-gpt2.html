A field report from inside small language models

Reaching Into GPT‑2

A chat window shows you the model's mouth. We spent a week on the other side: freezing its memories, shuffling them, rewiring three of its 144 attention heads, training it to forget. Every trace below is real output from those runs, not an illustration.

Act one

It recites a sentence it has only seen through a keyhole

GPT‑2 never read the sentence below. It was shown twenty scraps of three words each, one at a time, and we kept the memory it formed of the last word of every scrap: the numbers sitting inside the network at all twelve layers. Then we shuffled the pile, handed the model the first word, and let it write.

The scraps have no order and no positions. The model finds the next word by recognising, inside each scrap's memory, the two or three words that came before it. The circuit doing this is the same one that lets a chat model copy a name from earlier in your message; nobody had fed it a shuffled bag of memories before.

Single words do not work. Hand the model the same twenty words as isolated memories, with no neighbours, and the output collapses into filler, 16 percent of the words right by luck. A memory of a word is only usable if it was formed with a little context around it.

Act two

It changes its answer but never changes its mind

Tell GPT‑2 a fact, then correct it. The model gives the corrected answer. Inside, the old fact is still fully present; the model has only stopped looking at it. We can make it look again.

Three heads out of 144 carry the change of answer. Give those three their pre-correction state and the model reverts to the old city in 24 of 24 stories; three random heads manage it in 3 to 10. Repeating the old fact six times before the correction does not protect it. The model does not count evidence; it follows the grammar of "moved to".

Then we trained a copy of GPT‑2 to forget, with a loss that punishes any probability on the old city. On stories it never saw, the old city drops to zero. It did not erase anything: its heads now look at the old city 1 percent of the time instead of 15, and the old city's trace in the network survives at 63 percent of its untouched size. Rewire eight heads and the model says Paris again with certainty.

Act three

Its reading circuit was assigned at birth

The heads that did the work in the first two acts are the model's copy circuit, the part that finds an earlier word and reads what came after it. Which of the 48 heads in a small model become that circuit? We trained four models ourselves: two twins that started from identical random weights and read different books, and two more twins from a second set of random weights. Each square is one head; brightness is how strongly it acts as a copy head after 197 million words.

Twins share starting weights. Books are two different orderings of the same text.
When the circuit switched on, by training step

The same test on ten public Pythia‑160M models trained for 143,000 steps. Orange outline marks the strongest copy head.

Seven of these share the starting weights of the original Pythia‑160M and light up the same head, layer 4 head 10, whatever text they read. The three trained from their own random weights put the circuit somewhere else, and not in the same place as each other. One caveat we found the hard way: the three models published as "different initialization" turned out to share the original's starting weights after step zero, so they count as twins here, not strangers.

The dice thrown before training decide where the circuit will live. The text decides when it wakes up: both models on book two switched on around step 1,500 to 1,750, all three on book one at step 2,250, regardless of their starting weights.

At this tiny scale the birth's grip is partial. One pair of twins grew the circuit in the same head; the other pair only partly agreed. The public models, trained 25 times longer, agree almost perfectly.

Act four

Grammar gets stamped on the noun

Two stories differ by one word: "the museum then moved to London" and "moved from London". We swapped the memory of one word between the two stories, layer by layer, and watched whether the answer flipped. Swapping the memory of the city does it. Swapping the memory of the question does nothing until the very last layers, after the answer is already decided.

Share of stories whose answer flips after the swap, by layer (GPT‑2 small)

The word "to" is written onto "London" by layers 5 to 8, and that is where the copy heads read it. The question at the end of the story never carries the grammar itself.

Act five

The memory that erases itself

A newer kind of model, Qwen3.5, keeps part of its memory in a fixed-size register instead of a growing transcript. Every new word erases a little of what is stored before writing itself in. We measured what gets erased in a trained model, then switched the eraser off at inference and watched the cost.

Cross-entropy loss on 16 documents, lower is better

In its late blocks the register overwrites the previous word's memory 71 percent of the time. The eraser is not a cleaner that sweeps at the end of a sentence; it is a constant overwrite of the last few words along similar keys, and the model needs it.

What this changes about the chat window

The model does not read your message the way you read this page. It forms a memory of each word in the light of the two or three words before it, and those memories can be handled, shuffled, and handed back. When you correct it, it does not delete the old version; it looks away, and the old version can be looked at again. And which parts of it do the looking was settled by random numbers before it saw a single sentence.

How it was done: GPT‑2 small for acts one, two and four; four Pythia‑70M-shaped models trained from public initializations for act three, plus ten public Pythia‑160M checkpoints; Qwen3.5‑4B for act five. Memories are residual-stream states; rewiring is head-output patching at the answer position; the forgetting model is GPT‑2 fine-tuned for 400 steps on 16 entities and tested on 8 unseen ones. The exact composite forward used for act one reproduces ordinary next-word predictions to four decimals when given the real memories in order.
